The Mathematics of Lens Optics: Calculating Field of View (FOV) and Focal Length for Photography & Cinematography

Mastering camera optics requires a firm grasp of the mathematical relationship between lens focal length, sensor physical dimensions, and the resulting angle of view. Whether framing a cinematic sequence on a Super 35 sensor, calibrating a virtual 3D camera in Blender or Unreal Engine, or choosing the ideal prime lens for portrait photography, understanding how focal length dictates field of view is essential for creative control.

This professional optical calculator provides instant, precise conversions between field of view angles and lens focal lengths across various sensor formats. Below, we explore the underlying trigonometric formulas, examine sensor format size differences, and review standard lens classifications.

The Core Optical Formula: The field of view ($FOV$) angle is calculated using the sensor dimension ($d$) and focal length ($f$) via the tangent trigonometric function: $FOV = 2 \cdot \arctan \left( \frac{d}{2 \cdot f} \right)$. Conversely, solving for focal length yields $f = \frac{d}{2 \cdot \tan(FOV / 2)}$.

The Technical Core: Trigonometry, Sensors, and Rectilinear Projections

When light passes through a camera lens aperture, it projects an inverted image onto the digital image sensor or film plane. Several key optical factors govern this projection:

1. Sensor Format Dimensions

The physical width and height of the sensor dictate how much of the projected image circle is captured. A larger sensor (such as full frame 36x24mm) captures a wider field of view for the same focal length compared to a cropped sensor (such as APS-C or Micro Four Thirds).

2. Horizontal vs. Vertical vs. Diagonal FOV

Depending on composition requirements, cinematographers often analyze horizontal FOV (lateral perspective framing) or diagonal FOV (total corner-to-corner angle coverage). Our calculator computes horizontal, vertical, and focal parameters simultaneously.

3. Virtual Production & 3D Matching

In VFX pipelines and virtual production, matching live-action plates with CGI camera renders requires exact focal length matching. Converting known plate FOV angles to precise virtual camera focal lengths ensures seamless compositing without perspective distortion.
