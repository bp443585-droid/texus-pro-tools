The Technical Core: Mifflin-St Jeor Equation and Energy Expenditure

When calculating energy requirements, modern dietetics relies on validated mathematical models rather than generic population averages. Our calculator utilizes the Mifflin-St Jeor formula, widely recognized by clinical dietitians as the gold standard for predicting resting energy expenditure:

1. Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) Calculation

BMR represents the minimum number of calories your body requires at rest to sustain vital organ function, respiration, and cellular homeostasis in a thermoneutral environment:

  • For Men: $\text{BMR} = (10 \times \text{weight in kg}) + (6.25 \times \text{height in cm}) - (5 \times \text{age in years}) + 5$
  • For Women: $\text{BMR} = (10 \times \text{weight in kg}) + (6.25 \times \text{height in cm}) - (5 \times \text{age in years}) - 161$

2. Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) Scaling

Once BMR is established, the calculator multiplies your baseline figure by an activity factor coefficient ranging from 1.2 (sedentary office lifestyle) to 1.9 (heavy physical labor combined with intense athletic training) to determine your true daily caloric maintenance level.

3. Caloric Deficit Application

To initiate weight loss, a precise reduction is subtracted from your TDEE. A standard deficit of 500 kcal per day typically yields approximately 0.5 kg of weekly fat loss, balancing rapid progress with muscle retention and hormonal stability.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) - Nutritional Diagnostic Segment

What is the difference between BMR and TDEE?

BMR (Basal Metabolic Rate) is the energy expended strictly at rest just to keep you alive. TDEE (Total Daily Energy Expenditure) adds your physical activity, workouts, daily movement (NEAT), and the thermic effect of food (TEF) to your BMR, giving you your true daily calorie maintenance level.

Is a larger calorie deficit always better for faster weight loss?

No. Extreme calorie deficits (exceeding 1,000+ kcal daily) often cause rapid loss of lean muscle tissue, severe micronutrient deficiencies, hormonal disruptions (such as thyroid suppression and elevated cortisol), and metabolic adaptation, making long-term weight maintenance difficult.
