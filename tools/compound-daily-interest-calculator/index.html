The Core Mathematical Formula for Daily Compounding

To calculate the exact future value ($FV$) of an investment utilizing daily compounding with regular periodic contributions, financial engineers apply a modified continuous compounding formula tailored to discrete 365-day annual intervals:

1. Standard Compound Formula without Additional Contributions

When evaluating a lump-sum principal ($P$) invested at an annual nominal interest rate ($r$) for a duration of $t$ years with daily compounding ($n = 365$), the governing equation is:

FV = P × (1 + r / 365)^(365 × t)

2. Factoring in Daily Cash Flow Additions

When investors add regular daily contributions ($PMT$) alongside initial capital, the algorithm sums the future value of the initial principal with the future value of an ordinary annuity compounded daily across every discrete 24-hour cycle.

3. Nominal vs. Effective Annual Rate (EAR)

Because interest is credited every day, the effective annual yield is always slightly higher than the nominal stated interest rate. The Effective Annual Rate can be calculated via: $EAR = (1 + r/365)^{365} - 1$.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) - Expert Financial Guidance

Why does daily compounding yield higher returns than monthly compounding?

Daily compounding yields higher returns because interest is calculated and added to your principal 365 times a year instead of 12 times. This gives your earnings a shorter feedback loop, allowing your interest to start earning its own returns up to 30 times faster than monthly compounding schedules.

How do leap years affect daily compound interest calculations?

Standard financial software models assume a 365-day calendar year for consistency. However, exact institutional accounting algorithms adjust for leap years by dividing annual rates by 366 for every fourth calendar year. Our calculator models standard 365-day amortization schedules to provide reliable baseline projections.

Is daily compound interest taxable upon accrual or withdrawal?

In most standard taxable brokerage accounts and high-yield savings accounts, interest is considered taxable income in the year it is credited or made available to the account holder—even if the funds are left in the account to continue compounding. Always consult a certified tax professional regarding your specific jurisdiction.
