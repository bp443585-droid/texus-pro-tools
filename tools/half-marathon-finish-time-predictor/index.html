The Science of Endurance: Peter Riegel's Formula Explained

For decades, coaches and runners relied on rough guesswork to predict longer race outcomes. In 1981, runner and engineer Peter Riegel published a groundbreaking paper in Runner's World that mathematically codified the relationship between racing distance and finishing time.

1. The Riegel Equation Formula

The mathematical model is expressed as:
$T_2 = T_1 \times (D_2 / D_1)^{1.06}$
Where $T_1$ is your known time, $D_1$ is your known distance, $D_2$ is the target distance (21.0975 km), and $1.06$ is the universal fatigue degradation coefficient.

2. Accounting for Aerobic Decay

As running distance increases, muscle glycogen stores deplete, micro-tears accumulate in fast-twitch fibers, and thermoregulatory stress rises. The fatigue exponent ensures that predicted times realistically reflect the physiological slowdown experienced when doubling or tripling your racing distance.

3. Individualizing Your Fitness Baseline

While Riegel's formula serves as an exceptional gold standard, highly specialized runners—such as endurance-oriented athletes with high lactate thresholds—may experience a lower fatigue factor (closer to $1.04$), whereas speed-dominant runners transitioning from track events may see higher degradation ($1.08$).

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) - Expert Training Guidance

How accurate is this half marathon predictor for first-time runners?

The calculator is exceptionally accurate provided your baseline time represents a recent, all-out effort (such as a hard parkrun or scored race). If your benchmark time was set during a casual training run without race-day adrenaline and tapering, your actual half marathon performance will likely be faster!

Should I use negative splits or even pacing during my half marathon?

For 95% of runners, even pacing or slight negative splits (running the second half 30 to 60 seconds faster than the first) produce the fastest finishing times. Starting too fast triggers premature lactic acid accumulation that is nearly impossible to recover from past the 14-kilometer mark.

How many weeks of tapering are required before a half marathon?

Most intermediate training plans recommend a 2-week taper. Reduce your weekly mileage by 25-30% two weeks out, and cut mileage by 50% during race week while maintaining short race-pace intervals to keep your legs snappy.
