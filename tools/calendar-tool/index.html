Historical Evolution: From Julian Reformations to Gregorian Precision

The calendar we use today in the Western world and international business is the Gregorian calendar, introduced by Pope Gregory XIII in October 1582. Prior to this, the Julian calendar—established by Julius Caesar in 45 BCE—was the universal standard across the Roman Empire and medieval Europe.

1. The Julian Drift Problem

The Julian calendar assumed that a tropical solar year is precisely 365.25 days long, adding a single leap day every four years without exception. However, the true astronomical solar year (the time it takes Earth to complete one orbit around the Sun) is approximately 365.2422 days long. This slight discrepancy of roughly 11 minutes per year accumulated over centuries, causing the spring equinox to drift significantly off schedule and disrupting religious holiday calculations.

2. The 1582 Papal Correction

To realign the calendar with the solar year, Pope Gregory XIII decreed a correction where Thursday, October 4, 1582, was immediately followed by Friday, October 15, 1582, skipping 10 days instantly. Furthermore, the revised leap year algorithm omitted leap days in three out of every four century years to prevent future drift.

3. Global Adoption

While Catholic nations adopted the Gregorian reform immediately, Protestant and Orthodox countries transitioned much later. Great Britain and its American colonies made the switch in 1752 (losing 11 days), while Russia did not adopt the Gregorian calendar until 1918 following the Bolshevik revolution.

Why do some years have 365 days while others have 366 days?

Because Earth takes roughly 365 and a quarter days to orbit the sun, a standard 365-day year falls short of completing the solar cycle. Adding an extra day (February 29th) during leap years synchronizes our civil calendar back with astronomical reality.
