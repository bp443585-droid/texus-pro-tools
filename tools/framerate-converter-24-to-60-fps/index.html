The 2.5x Multiplier Ratio: Because 60 is exactly 2.5 times larger than 24 ($60 \div 24 = 2.5$), converting between these two frame standards requires careful distribution of interpolated frames to maintain uniform motion cadence across every single second of playback time.

The Mathematics of Temporal Interpolation: 24 to 60 FPS

When mapping a video stream from a lower cadence to a higher cadence, editors must choose between three distinct algorithmic approaches:

1. Nearest-Neighbor Frame Duplication (3:2 / 2:3 Pattern Equivalents)

In basic duplication models, source frames are repeated in a repeating cadence (e.g., repeating frame A three times, then frame B twice). While computationally instant and lossless regarding image sharpness, this method introduces noticeable motion judder and stutter during camera pans because time intervals between displayed images fluctuate.

2. Linear Frame Blending

Blending averages adjacent frames together with variable opacity to create intermediate transitional frames. While this softens abrupt jumps, it results in visible motion blur and ghosting artifacts, degrading visual fidelity in high-contrast scenes.

3. Optical Flow and AI Motion Estimation

Modern NLEs (Non-Linear Editors) and AI interpolation tools analyze pixel vectors between consecutive frames (Frame $N$ and Frame $N+1$) to synthesize brand new, highly accurate intermediate frames. This yields the smoothest possible 60 FPS result from 24 FPS source material.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ) - Technical Support Segment

Does converting 24 FPS to 60 FPS automatically make video look better?

Not necessarily. While 60 FPS provides smoother motion and is ideal for gaming, sports, and fast camera movement, 24 FPS carries the traditional "cinematic look" associated with movies and high-end narrative films. Converting 24 to 60 FPS using simple frame duplication can introduce "soap opera effect" motion artifacts unless high-quality optical flow algorithms are applied.

How does frame rate conversion affect audio synchronization?

When converting frame rates by changing the playback speed (e.g., speed ramping or time-stretching), audio pitch and duration can shift. However, when converting *only* the frame rate (framerate retiming without changing total duration), the audio timeline remains 100% locked to the video duration in seconds; only the underlying visual frame timestamps are remapped.
